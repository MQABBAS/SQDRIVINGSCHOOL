Young drivers and road safety: what 20 years of DfT data shows

Young driver deaths are down 75% since 2004, but 17–24-year-olds still face far higher risk. What the Department for Transport data shows — and how to learn safer.

SQ Driving Data LabPublished 16 October 20263 min readBy SQ Driving School · part of DriveSQ

Key findings

  • Young car drivers killed fell from 330 in 2004 to 84 — a 75% drop; serious injuries fell 63%.
  • A young driver was involved in around a fifth of killed-or-seriously-injured casualties from car collisions in 2023.
  • Male drivers aged 17–24 are about four times as likely to be killed or seriously injured as drivers aged 25+.
  • Six penalty points in your first two years means losing your licence and re-sitting both tests.
  • Experience of dark roads, fast roads and passengers before you pass is the best protection.

Passing your test is the start, not the finish. The Department for Transport's own figures show that newly qualified young drivers carry a much higher risk than everyone else on the road — but also that the picture has improved dramatically in 20 years. Here's what the data says, and what it means for how you should learn.

Twenty years of progress

Better cars, better roads and better training have all played a part. But 84 deaths and 1,297 serious injuries a year are still 84 families and 1,297 lives changed — and young drivers remain over-represented.

Why young drivers are still at higher risk

  • About a fifth of serious casualties. In 2023 a young driver was involved in around one in five of all killed or seriously injured casualties from collisions involving cars — far more than their share of drivers or miles driven.
  • Young men especially. Male drivers aged 17–24 were around four times as likely to be killed or seriously injured while driving as car drivers aged 25 and over.
  • Per mile, not just per head. Young (and the oldest) drivers have higher killed-or-seriously-injured rates per mile driven than drivers in the middle age groups — so it isn't just that they drive more.

Research into young driver collisions consistently points to the same pressure points: inexperience spotting hazards early, night-time driving, carrying friends as passengers, speed and fast rural roads. None of these is fully tested in a 40-minute driving test — which is exactly why how you learn matters.

The New Drivers Act: six points and you're out

For your first two years after passing, the rules are stricter. If you build up six or more penalty points within two years of passing your test, your licence is revoked and you go back to being a learner — provisional licence, theory test and practical test all over again. One mobile-phone offence (six points) is enough on its own.

Quick maths: a speeding fine is usually 3 points. Two of them in your first two years and you're re-sitting your theory test.

What the data means for how you learn

The test measures a minimum standard on one day. The DfT figures are a reminder that real safety comes from experience in the conditions where young drivers get hurt. With SQ Driving School, that means:

  • Dark and low-light driving before you pass — especially after the clocks change (see our daylight study).
  • Fast roads and dual carriageways such as the M60 and A-roads around Greater Manchester, so speed and merging aren't new after your test.
  • Hazard perception that goes beyond the theory test — try our spot-the-hazard game.
  • Honest progress tracking in the DriveSQ Student Portal, so you book your test when you're ready, not just when a slot appears.

After your test, our new driver's guide covers your first months on the road, and our insurance guide explains how telematics policies reward safe driving.

Frequently asked questions

How many young drivers are killed on UK roads each year?
The Department for Transport's latest younger driver factsheet records 84 car drivers aged 17–24 killed and 1,297 seriously injured, down from 330 and 3,481 in 2004.
Are young drivers more likely to crash?
Yes. In 2023 a young driver (17–24) was involved in around a fifth of all killed or seriously injured casualties from collisions involving cars, and young male drivers were about four times as likely to be killed or seriously injured as drivers aged 25 and over.
What happens if you get 6 points in your first 2 years of driving?
Under the New Drivers Act your licence is revoked. You have to apply for a new provisional licence and pass both the theory and practical tests again.
How can new drivers reduce their risk?
Get experience in the conditions linked to young driver collisions — dark roads, fast roads, bad weather and driving with passengers — before and after passing, keep speed down and never use a phone while driving.
